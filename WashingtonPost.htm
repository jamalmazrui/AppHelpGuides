The Washington Post Help Guide

This guide gathers The Washington Post’s own help documentation as it stood on 3 August 2026. The text is the Post’s; nothing has been summarised or rewritten.

This is not the news. Nothing here tells you what happened yesterday. It is the documentation for using the Post: subscriptions and billing, signing in, the apps, print delivery, newsletters and notifications, the crossword and games, commenting, and accessibility. A file named for a newspaper is easy to mistake for the newspaper, so it is worth saying plainly at the top.

The surfaces it covers. The Post publishes help for its website, its iPhone and iPad apps, its Android app and its print edition, and all of it is here under the Post’s own headings. There is no separate Windows application — the Post on Windows is the website in a browser, and the website help is what applies. The Post’s Alexa material is thin: it offers a flash briefing rather than a full skill, and there is no dedicated help section for it in this centre. Where the Post’s own articles name a platform they say so; where an article does not, it holds for every surface.

Accessibility has a category of its own, which is unusual and worth knowing about — eleven articles covering screen reader use, text size, contrast, keyboard navigation and closed captioning on video.

The arrangement is the Post’s own, taken from its help centre rather than invented here.

What was left out. One article on how to advertise with the Post, which is written for businesses buying from it rather than for its readers. The Group Subscriptions material is kept, because a reader arriving through a university or an employer needs it to sign in.

This document holds 8 categories, 95 articles and about 15,272 words.

How do I access Podcast transcripts?

Updated 2023-06-07

We release a transcript with every podcast in an effort to make it accessible to all our users.

If there is no “transcript” button visible, there may still be a transcript available. Click the “Options” button for the podcast, and then click “View transcript.” A pop-up will appear with the transcript for the podcast, as shown below.

How do I listen to an article?

Updated 2023-06-07

Our “Listen” button (shown below on both desktop and mobile screen sizes) plays the base text content of an article as audio. Please note this feature is not available on all articles.

The “Listen” button is not a substitute for using a screen reader. For example, the button does not play audio of alt text for images. We recommend that users who are blind or have low vision instead use a screen reader for full functionality.

How to use gift articles

Updated 2025-05-28

What is a gift article?

All Washington Post subscribers can now gift up to 10 free articles a month to anyone who does not have a Washington Post subscription. This new benefit is intended to help subscribers easily and securely share articles with friends, family, and colleagues that are not subscribers.

The recipient of a gift article can read it for free for up to 14 days, without having to subscribe The Post. Gift article recipients will need to register for a Washington Post account.

How can I gift an article?

When viewing an article on the Washington Post website, select the share button, which is displayed above the article’s first paragraph.

Once you select the share button, if you still have gift articles remaining, there are several options for gifting the article, including copying a link, sending an email, or sharing on social media platforms like Facebook and Twitter. The share button appears a second time at the end of each article and can be used in the same way. You can gift an article through the Washington Post website and apps on any device. This feature is not currently available on Apple News.

On the mobile app, please tap on the three-dot menu icon to select the gift icon, and then the “Share gift article” button to view your gifting options.

How many articles can I gift?

You can gift up to 10 articles each calendar month. Each gifted article can be shared with multiple recipients for up to 14 days.

Your gift article count will not decrease when you first select the gift icon. When you select a share option (such as copying a link or sending an email), a special link is generated that counts as one of your 10 gift articles for the month.

How do I know if I’ve reached my gifted article limit?

When you have used all 10 of your gift articles for the month, you will see a message asking you to check back next month for 10 more. You can still share articles as you normally would, but non-subscribers may not be able to access them freely.

Can I gift an article by simply sharing it on social media or copying the link from my browser’s address bar?

No. The only way to gift an article and provide free access for non-subscribers is through the share button. Other methods of sharing will not generate the same kind of special link that provides free access for non-subscribers

For Gift Article Recipients

I’ve received a link to a gift article. What does this mean?

Articles that are gifted by current subscribers, through the share button on the Washington Post website, are freely available to non-subscribers. Articles that are shared through other methods, such as forwarding an email, may therefore be restricted.

Do I need to register or sign-in to read the free article?

Yes, you will need to register or sign in to access the free gifted article. This will be prompted upon opening the gift link.

How long do I have free access to this article?

A recipient of a gift article can access the story for 14 days.

I received a link to an article, but it doesn’t mention that it’s a gift.

Articles that are gifted by current subscribers, through the gift article icon on the Washington Post website, are freely available to non-subscribers. Articles that are shared through other methods, such as forwarding an email may be restricted.

How to use Sign in with Apple

Updated 2024-08-01

New users

Sign in with Apple is available to any Washington Post reader creating a free account or paid subscription for the first time. New users can choose to share or hide their Apple ID email.

  1. On the Washington Post Sign Up page, tap the Apple sign-in button; you’ll be taken to an Apple sign-in page.

  2. Enter your Apple ID and password

  3. You may need to provide two-factor authentication. Follow the steps to complete this.

  4. Review the page “Sign in with Apple,” and tap Continue

  5. Next, you will see a page asking if you’d like to “Create an account for The Washington Post using your Apple ID”. Review the default selections

    • Share my email will share your email with The Washington Post
    • Hide my email will hide your email from The Washington Post and share a unique Relay Email

  7. Tap continue to return to washingtonpost.com.

Existing Users

To connect Sign in with Apple to an existing account or subscription, you must choose, Share my email. Your Apple ID email must match the one on your Washington Post account, or we will not be able to connect the two.

  1. On the Washington Post Sign Up page, tap the Apple sign-in button; you’ll be taken to an Apple sign-in page.
  2. Enter your Apple ID and password
  3. You may need to provide two-factor authentication. Follow the steps to complete this.
  4. Review the page “Sign in with Apple,” and tap Continue
  5. Next, you will see a page asking if you’d like to “Create an account for The Washington Post using your Apple ID.” You must select Share my email, and your Apple ID email must match the one on your Washington Post account.
  6. Tap continue to return to washingtonpost.com. Your Apple ID is now connected to your Washington Post account.

A 30-day pass provides one person with 30 days of unlimited digital access to The Washington Post, at no additional cost to you or them. This is a free benefit included with Home Delivery and Premium subscriptions. One 30-day pass may be shared each month.

Home Delivery subscriptions and Premium subscriptions include the benefits of a 30-day pass and three extra accounts to share with friends and family. All Home Delivery subscriptions include a Premium subscription. Please contact our Help Center if you’d like to upgrade your subscription.

Can my 30-day pass be shared with the same person more than one time?

Yes, you may share your 30-day pass with anyone you like. You receive one 30-day pass per month.

Does my 30-day pass expire?

Yes, passes must be shared before the end of the month. If you forget, don’t worry — you’ll have another pass coming the next month.

Submit an op-ed

Updated 2026-04-14

Please use our op-ed submission form.

What’s an op-ed?

Taking its name from its traditional position opposite the editorial page of a newspaper, an op-ed is an opinion essay written by a staff columnist or an outside contributor. It should have a clear point of view or argument supported by specific evidence. It does not represent the opinions of The Washington Post — in fact, it may often contradict the opinion of The Post’s Editorial Board.

What are the guidelines for op-ed submissions?

Submissions should be limited to 800 words. We consider only completed articles and cannot commit to, or provide guidance on, article proposals. Op-eds may not have been submitted to, posted to or published by any other media. They must include the writer’s full name — anonymous op-eds or op-eds written under pseudonyms will not be considered. They also must include the writer’s home address, email address and telephone numbers. Additionally, we ask that writers disclose any personal or financial interest in the subject at hand.

How are op-ed contributions selected?

The Post receives hundreds of submissions a day and on most days can accommodate only a handful of outside contributions in print and online. Among the things we look for are timeliness (is it pegged to something in the news?), resonance (is it something that will interest Post readers?) and freshness of perspective (is it an argument we haven’t heard many times before?). You don’t need to have special expertise in a topic. But explaining how your background or experience informs your point of view can make for a more effective op-ed. You also don’t need to have an important title — and having an important title doesn’t mean we’ll publish your op-ed. In fact, because we realize that senators, business leaders, heads of state and the like have access to various platforms where they can express their views, we hold them to a particularly high standard when considering whether to publish them in The Post.

When will I hear back about my op-ed submission?

We do our best to read all submissions promptly. If you don’t hear from us within a week, it’s safe to assume we won’t be able to use your op-ed.

Are op-eds edited?

Our editors are careful not to alter a writer’s opinions or “voice,” but all op-eds are edited for clarity and precision of language and for logic of argumentation and organization. They are also fact checked and copy edited for grammar and style and may be adjusted to fit the space available in the newspaper. We ask contributors to sign off on all changes before we publish a final version on the website or op-ed page.

Are op-eds solicited?

Although we carefully review all unsolicited pieces that are submitted, we also reach out to people we think might have an interesting opinion on a topic in the news — especially when the news is breaking and we’d like to get smart commentary onto the website and into the paper quickly.

Why do I have to sign in every time I want to read a Post article on social media?

Updated 2024-05-02

When you tap on a Washington Post article you see in a social media app, you’ll usually be asked to sign in to The Post each time — even if you’re a subscriber and signed in elsewhere.

Why it happens

Many apps — including Facebook, Instagram, X, and LinkedIn — use their own in-app browsers that allow you to visit other websites (like The Post) without actually leaving their app. These browsers are different from default browsers that come installed on your phone (e.g. Chrome, Safari, Edge).

In-app browsers don’t save your sign-in details for various reasons. Because of this, you may need to sign in every time to read an article you see in an app (other than The Washington Post app).

The app makers have full control over their in-app browsers, which means The Post does not have any control over the experience.

Ways to improve your experience

  • Read in The Washington Post app instead (where you always stay signed in).

  • On Facebook, you can choose to open the article in your phone’s default browser, which may keep you signed in for longer periods of time. Here’s how:

    • iOS: Tap the ellipsis (“…”) at the bottom of the screen and then “Open in system browser.”

    • Android: Tap the ellipsis (“…) at the top of the screen and then “Open in browser.”

    • Android Facebook readers can also set links to open externally on their phone’s default browser. In the Android Facebook app, go to Settings and Privacy > Settings > Preferences (Media) > select “Links open externally.”
  • Use a password manager to make signing in faster and easier.
  • Double-check that your subscription is active. Visit Subscription & Billing in My Post to confirm your subscription status.
