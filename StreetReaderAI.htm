StreetReaderAI Help Guide

StreetReaderAI is a web app from Google Research that lets blind and low-vision people explore real streets by ear. You stand at a spot on a street, turn, step forward, and hear what is around you: the roads, the nearby places, and an AI description of the scene. You can also ask questions out loud or by typing, such as whether there is a marked crosswalk ahead.

About this guide. Gathered 27 November 2026 from what Google Research has published:

  • The StreetReaderAI project on GitHub, its read-me page and its settings document
  • The Google Research blog post about the original prototype and its study
  • The research paper, presented at the UIST 2025 conference

StreetReaderAI runs in a web browser, so it works on Windows. Its project calls it an experimental research prototype that will likely have bugs, meant for demonstration rather than everyday travel. The developer documents (the programming reference and the routing design) are left out.

The open-source version differs a little from the prototype that Google tested in 2025. Where they differ, this guide follows the open-source version, because that is the one you can run.

About StreetReaderAI

Limits and plans

StreetReaderAI is a proof of concept. Keep these limits in mind:

  • It is not built for real travel decisions, and the project says it is for demonstration only.
  • It does not plan a full trip from a starting point to a destination.
  • Speech is its main output. Richer sounds are still being explored.
  • The AI can be wrong. In the 2025 study, most wrong answers were the AI saying something was not there when it was, such as a bike rack.

Google Research has described these future ideas:

  • An agent that explores on its own and answers questions like “What’s the next bus stop down this road?”
  • An agent that walks a route ahead of you and sums it up, including obstacles and building entrances.
  • Sound placed around you in space, made from the images.

What testers found

In 2025, eleven blind screen reader users tried the first prototype in a lab.

  • They rated its overall usefulness 6.4 out of 7.
  • They valued having movement and AI together, the chat, and the information it gave.
  • They looked at over 350 street views and made over 1,000 AI requests. They used AI Chat six times as often as AI Describer.
  • Their most common questions were about which way things were, whether something was there, what the scene looked like, and where a thing was.
  • Of 816 questions, 703 (86.3%) were answered correctly, 26 partly, 54 were declined, and 32 were wrong.
  • Some testers found it hard to keep their bearings, to judge whether an answer was right, and to know the limits of what the AI knew.

Who made it

Jon E. Froehlich, Alex Fiannaca, Victor Tsaran, Nimer Jaber and Shaun Kane built the original prototype. Shaun Kane, Sam Sepah, Bill Neubauer and Philip Nelson made the open-source version. Google Research says the prototype was designed by blind and sighted accessibility researchers working together. StreetReaderAI is free and open source under the Apache 2.0 license.

AI features

AI Chat

AI Chat answers questions about what is in front of you, what you passed earlier, and the area nearby. Each time you turn or move, the app sends the current view and your location details to the AI.

  1. Press Mod+K to move to the chat box. (Mod is Alt unless you change it.)
  2. Type a question, such as “Is there a marked crosswalk?”, and press Enter.
  3. Or press Mod+B, or choose the microphone button, and ask aloud.

Chat remembers earlier parts of your session. In the prototype, a tester could walk past a bus stop, turn a corner, and ask where the bus stop was.

If movement commands are turned on in Settings, you can also tell the chat “turn left” or “move forward.”

AI Describer

AI Describer gives a short description, two or three sentences, of the scene in front of you: the street layout, sidewalks, buildings and landmarks. It joins what the picture shows with nearby places and roads.

  • Press Mod+A to turn automatic descriptions on or off. When they are on, you hear a new description as you move.
  • You can also choose automatic descriptions in Settings.

The original prototype had a second style, a tour guide that added history and building details. The open-source read-me does not mention it.

Preset questions

When you are not typing in a box, press 5, 6 or 7 to ask one of the app’s ready questions about the view.

Getting started

Choosing where pictures and places come from

StreetReaderAI takes its street pictures and its place names from outside services. You choose them in the settings file, streetreaderai.conf.

Street pictures:

  • Panoramax (the default): free, open 360-degree pictures. No key needed.
  • Mapillary: needs a free token from the Mapillary Developer Portal.
  • Google Street View: needs a Google Maps key.

Places and addresses:

  • OpenStreetMap (the default): no key needed.
  • Google: needs a Google Maps key.

Not every street has pictures from every service, so try another service if a place has none.

Getting an AI key

StreetReaderAI needs one AI service to describe scenes and answer questions:

  • Google Gemini (the default): get a key from the API key page of Google AI Studio. In AI Studio itself, the app finds the key on its own.
  • OpenAI: get a key from OpenAI’s platform site.

Put your key in the settings file under the keys section, and set the AI choice to match. Your keys stay on the computer running the app. They are not sent to the web browser.

The project does not say exactly what is sent to each AI service, so read each service’s terms before you use it.

Running it in Google AI Studio

This way needs no setup on your computer.

  1. Open Google AI Studio and go to its Build section.
  2. Import the public StreetReaderAI project from GitHub.
  3. Run it. AI Studio supplies the Gemini key for you.

To share it, use Share for a link, or Deploy to Cloud Run for a web address of its own. In AI Studio, the app speaks with your computer’s own voice, which avoids using up cloud speech allowances.

Running it on your own computer

You need Node.js version 18 or later. Then:

  1. Download the project from GitHub.
  2. In its folder, run make setup to install what it needs.
  3. Edit streetreaderai.conf to choose your services and add your keys.
  4. Run make start.
  5. Open http://localhost:4200 in your browser.

After you change the settings file, refresh the browser page. You do not need to restart. If a key is missing, a Configuration Error dialog names it.

The Welcome and Settings dialogs

The first time you open StreetReaderAI, a Welcome dialog asks how you want it to work. Change these later with the Settings button.

  • Self-voicing speech: the app speaks for itself. Turn this off if you use NVDA, JAWS or VoiceOver, so you hear your screen reader instead.
  • Automatic descriptions: a new scene description as you move.
  • Chat movement commands: lets you say or type “turn left” and the like.
  • Modifier key: the Mod key in the shortcuts. It is Alt unless you change it.
  • Direction format: directions as compass points, degrees, or both.
  • Voice type: Gemini speech, or Local, which uses the English voices on your computer. If Gemini speech fails, you hear the error in the local voice.

Moving around

Finding your bearings

  • Press Mod+I to hear your current address, which way you face, and the streets nearby.
  • After you turn, the app says which way you face, such as North or Northeast.
  • After a step, it says how far you went and whether you can keep going.
  • Nearby businesses and landmarks are named by where they sit from you, such as FRONT LEFT or BEHIND RIGHT.

Jumping to a place

  • Press Mod+L to move to the address search. Type an address, pick from the suggestions, and press Enter.
  • Or, when you are not typing, press 1, 2, 3 or 4 to jump to a ready-made place: Seattle, Paris, Amsterdam or Lyon.

Moving and turning

When you are not typing in a box:

  • Up Arrow: step forward about 18 meters (about 60 feet).
  • Down Arrow: turn around.
  • Left Arrow or Right Arrow: turn 45 degrees left or right.

Each of these also works with the Mod key held, such as Mod+Up Arrow. That is handy if your screen reader would otherwise take the arrow keys.

In the original prototype, Down Arrow stepped backward instead of turning around.

Miscellaneous

Getting help and reporting problems

  • Press the question mark key (?) in the app to open its help and shortcut list.
  • Report bugs on the project’s GitHub issues page.
  • Write to the team at streetreaderai-team@google.com.

Keyboard shortcuts

Mod is Alt unless you change it in Settings. Number keys and plain arrows work when you are not typing in a box.

  • Up Arrow, or Mod+Up Arrow: step forward.
  • Down Arrow, or Mod+Down Arrow: turn around.
  • Left or Right Arrow, or Mod with either: turn 45 degrees.
  • 1 to 4: jump to a ready-made place.
  • 5 to 7: ask a preset question.
  • Mod+A: turn automatic descriptions on or off.
  • Mod+B: ask by voice.
  • Mod+I: hear your address, direction and nearby streets.
  • Mod+K: move to the chat box.
  • Mod+L: move to the address search.
  • Mod+T: screen curtain.
  • ? (question mark): open help and shortcuts.
  • Escape: stop speech and close dialogs.

The screen curtain

Press Mod+T for the screen curtain, which blacks out everything shown on the screen. Your screen reader and the app’s speech keep working.
