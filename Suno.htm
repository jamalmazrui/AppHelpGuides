Can I get a refund?

Edited

There are a few key areas in our Terms of Service regarding refunds.

From Your Registration Obligations

If we discontinue the Service, we will refund you any pre-paid fees on a pro rata basis, based upon the number of months left in the pre-payment period.

This indicates that we will offer a refund in cases where we discontinue service. As noted, the refund will be prorated based on the remaining subscription duration.

From Refunds and Cancellations

Payments made by you hereunder are final and non-refundable, unless otherwise determined by Suno.

These are refunds made on a case-by-case basis. Some examples of reasons we would honor a refund would include:

  • Accidental payment: if you paid for a yearly subscription instead of a month, we can help to revert your payment to a monthly plan. This would include a partial refund.

  • Accidental/unattended sign up: If you started a subscription but didn’t use it at all. This can be tricky to determine; but if there are zero songs in your account and we see one or more payments, we’re likely to assist with a refund.

  • Product malfunction: If you experience significant downtime due to an outage or feature that isn’t working, we’ll go above and beyond to offer credits to make up for it. Unlike monthly credits, these will carry over to the next month! Some cases where we would not honor a refund:

  • Changed your mind: You’ve used Suno for six months, and decided now that you don’t like it. In this case, our team will recommend you cancel your plan. You can continue to use the service through the end of the billing cycle, and the account will revert to the Basic plan upon completion.

    • If it’s only been a day or two, give our team a shout at support@suno.com and we’ll do our best to make it right!

  • Intermittent usage: If you’ve had a monthly plan and used Suno on and off for several months, we cannot issue a refund for the time you didn’t use it. Instead, we encourage you to cancel anytime you won’t be using Suno, and resubscribe when you’re ready to use more than the Basic plan credit allotment! refund

What happens if I use all of my paid credits?

Edited

If you’re using a Pro or Premier plan, credits are replenished once monthly at the same time as your original purchase. For example, if you subscribed on July 4th at 3pm local time, your next replenishment will happen at 3pm local time on August 4th.

If you run out of monthly credits, you will receive the same 50 credit daily allotment as our Basic plan users, starting on the next day and running to the end of the billing cycle. When a new cycle begins, your monthly allotment will be used first, and the 50 daily credits will not reach your account until all monthly credits are used.

Beyond the 50 extra daily credits, you can purchase additional credits by visiting suno.com/account then choosing Buy More Credits to purchase add-on credits to be used while you are subscribed.

Note: Songs made with the 50 credits per day will still be granted commercial use rights, as long as you are still subscribed. Once your membership reverts to the Basic tier, any new songs will not be eligible for commercial use.

This document provides information for California consumers, pursuant to California Civil Code Section 3110 et seq., on how we, Suno, Inc. and our affiliates and subsidiaries (“Suno”), use data to train Suno’s generative artificial intelligence services.

Intended purpose: Suno uses the collected data to train its text-to-speech generative AI models, which are intended to create novel speech and other sounds from text prompts.

Data points, including counts and types: Suno’s training data consists of millions of public audio files and corresponding textual metadata that help teach the models about what speech (and other sounds) sound like.

Inclusion of public domain or IP-protected data: Suno trains its models on datasets that may be from the public domain and/or that may be subject to intellectual property protection.

Acquisition of data: As described above, Suno’s models were developed with publicly-available data, obtained in a manner that abided by all paywalls, password protections, and the like.

Data collection time period: Suno has been collecting data to train its text-to-speech generative AI models since Fall 2022.

Dates the datasets were first used: Suno began collecting the datasets in Spring 2023 and began using them for model development shortly thereafter.

Upcoming Changes FAQ: Downloads, Models, and Terms of Service

Edited

As we continue to build, we want to provide transparency around upcoming changes to how you use Suno. Our new Terms of Service can be found here, and we’re including an FAQ below to help preview the changes coming in September 2026.

  • How do I download my songs?
    • From your Library or Workspace, click the triple-dot (…) icon, and hover over Download to see download options and make your selection. To learn more about downloading, watch this video tutorial.
  • Why do downloads matter?
    • We believe a healthy music ecosystem is built on human creativity. As our platform grows, limiting downloads will make it harder for bad actors to mass-export music. We are introducing download limits to ensure our platform is used for intentional music creation, while still allowing everyone to create, remix, listen, and share on Suno.
  • How many downloads does each tier get?
    • Free: Up to 7 total (lifetime) trial downloads for personal, non-commercial use only
    • Pro: 20 downloads per month, with commercial use rights
    • Premier: 60 downloads per month, with commercial use rights
    • Monthly downloads reset on your billing date. Streaming and sharing on Suno remain unlimited on every plan. For Premier users on Suno Studio, our professional tool for generating and manipulating stems and samples, you can continue downloading without disrupting your workflow.
  • Can users still listen to and share their full library in-app?
    • Yes. Every song in your library stays fully playable and shareable through links on Suno, on every plan including free. These limits only apply to downloading files off the platform. Nothing changes about listening to, sharing, or linking your music on Suno.
  • What exactly counts as one download?
    • One song counts as one download, regardless of format. For example, if you download a song twice, that counts as one download. Failed or interrupted downloads don’t count against your quota. Stems from a song count as part of that song’s download, not as separate downloads.
  • Do the new download restrictions apply to the songs I’ve already made in Suno?
    • Download limits apply to all music on Suno starting September 3, including songs created before that date. Your existing library is not going anywhere: every song you’ve made stays fully playable and shareable on Suno, on every plan including free. The limits only apply to downloading files off the platform.
  • Does downloading the same song twice count towards my quota?
    • No. Once you’ve downloaded a song, downloading it again in any format, doesn’t count against your quota.
  • Does downloading the individual stems from the same song count as individual downloads?
    • No. All stems from a song are part of that song’s single download. Downloading a song plus its stems counts once, not per file.
  • Can I purchase more downloads?
    • Yes. For users who hit their download limits, they will be able to purchase additional downloads.
  • Do unused monthly downloads roll over?
    • No. Pro and Premier download counts reset each month and unused downloads don’t carry over.
  • How do download restrictions impact my Suno Studio workflow?
    • For those using Suno Studio, our professional production suite for generating and manipulating stems and samples, users will still be able to download their work from Studio without limitation. We want to maintain all professional workflows.
  • How do trial downloads work?
    • Each free account comes with up to 7 trial downloads. This means that you can download up to 7 songs to your device before deciding whether to upgrade for more downloads. Trial downloads are lifetime downloads, and don’t reset monthly like downloads do on Pro and Premier plans. You do not get commercial rights to trial downloaded songs.
  • I paid for an annual plan that included unlimited downloads. What are my options?
    • If you no longer wish to be a subscriber, you can cancel your plan by heading to suno.com/account. Read more about how to cancel your plan here.
  • What happens if I cancel my subscription?
    • If you cancel your subscription, your library stays yours and all of your songs remain in your account. You can listen and share them on Suno on the free tier. However, downloads follow free tier limits.
  • Do I still have commercial rights to my music?

Advanced Stem Separation

Making Music

Advanced Stem Separation

Edited

Advanced Stem Separation breaks a song down with more precision and cleaner results than before, and it lets you decide how that happens with three unique separation modes: Auto Split, Split from Mix, and Advanced Split. Each built for a different separation goal as explained below. Strip out a vocal, lift a single instrument, or prep a full set of stems for your DAW. The mode you choose shapes how the song comes apart and which pieces you walk away with. Accessing these split tiers requires a paid Suno plan. Pro members can utilize the Auto Split and Split from Mix features, whereas those on the Premier tier gain additional access to the Advanced mode.

Choosing how to split your song
Auto Split

Auto Split analyzes your track and divides it into up to 12 stems — drums, bass, guitar, keyboards, woodwinds, and more. Auto Split uses 50 credits per extraction (12 stems).

Split from Mix

Split from Mix lets you extract a single instrument or vocal from your song — for example, if you want to separate just the bassline, vocal or guitar part from the song. You also get the complement to your split, containing everything except the extracted stem. Split from Mix uses 10 credits per extraction (20 credits total for both stems created).

Advanced Split

Advanced Split lets you decide which stems your track gets separated into. Choose from a list of nearly 100 instruments and create your custom stems to suit your track. Advanced Split uses 10 credits per extraction (20 credits total for both stems created), per stem.

How to Use Advanced Stem Separation on Suno

Best use cases
  • Auto Split: Quickly separate stems of your song for mixing in Studio
  • Split from Mix: Separate specific stems for a performance track; i.e.a singer who wants an instrumental made to perform live
  • Search quickly through stems to separate for a faster workflow
What changed from the old version

Before this update, you had two choices: Auto and Vocal + Instrumental.

  • Auto shown as “all detected stems” is still around. It recognizes up to 12 instrument types, though it doesn’t always label them correctly.
  • Vocal + Instrumental has been updated to Split from Mix - with the ability to choose which instrument you split (not just vocal) The new modes push that idea much further, especially for producers who need to cleanly isolate one instrument at a time.

Stem separation is a paid feature, so you’ll need a Suno subscription to use it. As a Pro subscriber, you get Auto and Split from Mix. Premier subscribers get those two options, plus Advanced mode.

Note: If you try to split out an instrument or vocal that is not actually present in the song, the request may still use credits, but it may not produce a usable stem.

Music Glossary for Suno

Making Music

Music Glossary for Suno

Edited

Stuck on a prompt and want to try some musical terms? Maybe you need some inspiration to get what you want but want a new way to describe it? We’ve got you covered with our glossary of music terms to try out in Suno.

Tempo & Rhythm

Tempo - The speed of a piece of music, measured in beats per minute (BPM)

Adagio - Slow tempo (66-76 BPM), meaning “at ease”

Allegro - Fast, lively tempo (120-168 BPM)

Andante - Moderate walking pace (76-108 BPM)

Presto - Very fast tempo (168-200 BPM)

Rubato - Flexible tempo where the performer speeds up and slows down expressively

Syncopation - Rhythmic emphasis on normally weak beats or off-beats

Polyrhythm - Two or more conflicting rhythms played simultaneously

Groove - The rhythmic feel or “pocket” that makes you want to move

Downbeat - The first beat of a measure, typically the strongest

Upbeat - The beat before the downbeat, often creating anticipation

Dynamics & Expression

Dynamics - The volume or intensity of sound in music

Crescendo - Gradually getting louder

Diminuendo/Decrescendo - Gradually getting softer

Forte (f) - Loud

Piano (p) - Soft

Fortissimo (ff) - Very loud

Pianissimo (pp) - Very soft

Accent - Emphasis on a particular note or beat

Staccato - Short, detached notes

Legato - Smooth, connected notes

Vibrato - A slight variation in pitch that adds warmth and expression

Tremolo - Rapid repetition of a note or alternation between notes

Song Structure

Verse - Sections that tell the story, typically with changing lyrics

Chorus - The main, repeated section with the central message or hook

Bridge - A contrasting section that provides variety and builds tension

Pre-Chorus - A transitional section building up to the chorus

Intro - The opening section that establishes the mood

Outro - The closing section that ends the song

Hook - A catchy, memorable musical or lyrical phrase

Refrain - A repeated line or phrase, often at the end of verses

Break - A section where some instruments drop out, creating contrast

Drop - In electronic music, the moment of maximum energy release

Melody & Harmony

Melody - The main tune or sequence of notes that stands out

Harmony - Notes played simultaneously that support the melody

Chord - Three or more notes played together

Chord Progression - A sequence of chords that forms the harmonic foundation

Key - The tonal center of a piece based on a specific scale

Major - A key or chord with a bright, happy sound

Minor - A key or chord with a darker, sadder sound

Scale - A sequence of notes in ascending or descending order

Interval - The distance between two pitches

Octave - The interval between one note and another with double its frequency

Arpeggio - Playing chord notes in sequence rather than simultaneously

Counterpoint - Two or more independent melodies played together

Dissonance - Tension created by clashing notes

Resolution - Movement from dissonance to consonance, creating satisfaction

Genres & Styles

Blues - Genre characterized by specific chord progressions and expressive vocals

Jazz - Genre featuring improvisation, swing rhythms, and complex harmonies

Rock - Guitar-driven genre with strong backbeat

Pop - Accessible, catchy music aimed at mainstream audiences

Electronic/EDM - Music created primarily with electronic instruments and computers

Hip-Hop - Genre featuring rap vocals, sampling, and strong beats

R&B - Rhythm and blues with soulful vocals and groove-oriented arrangements

Country - Genre with roots in American folk, often featuring acoustic instruments

Classical - Art music tradition spanning centuries with formal structures

Folk - Traditional music passed down through communities

Funk - Groove-based genre with syncopated basslines and rhythmic emphasis

Soul - Emotive genre combining gospel, R&B, and blues elements

Reggae - Jamaican genre with offbeat rhythms and social themes

Metal - Heavy, aggressive rock with distorted guitars and powerful vocals

Ambient - Atmospheric music focused on texture and mood over traditional structure

Instrumentation & Texture

Instrumentation - The specific instruments used in a piece

Arrangement - How different instruments and parts are organized

Texture - The overall sound quality created by combining different elements

Monophonic - Single melodic line without accompaniment

Homophonic - Melody with harmonic accompaniment

Polyphonic - Multiple independent melodic lines

Orchestration - The art of assigning musical elements to specific instruments

Timbre - The unique color or quality of a sound (what makes a guitar sound different from a piano)

Layering - Stacking multiple sounds or instruments for richness

Sparse - Minimal instrumentation with space between elements

Dense - Many instruments or elements playing simultaneously

Vocal Techniques

Falsetto - High, airy vocal register above normal range

Belt - Powerful, sustained singing in chest voice at high pitches

Melisma - Singing multiple notes on a single syllable

Vocal Run - Quick succession of notes, often improvised

Harmonization - Multiple voices singing different notes simultaneously

A Cappella - Singing without instrumental accompaniment

Call and Response - Musical conversation where one phrase is answered by another

Scat - Improvised vocal improvisation using nonsense syllables (common in jazz)

Crooning - Soft, intimate singing style

Rapping - Rhythmic spoken or chanted lyrics

Production & Effects

Reverb - Effect simulating sound in a space (room, hall, cathedral)

Delay/Echo - Repetition of sound after a time interval

Compression - Reducing the dynamic range between loud and quiet sounds

Distortion - Intentional alteration of sound, often making it grittier or heavier

Filter - Effect that removes or emphasizes certain frequencies

Modulation - Variation in pitch, amplitude, or other parameters

Panning - Positioning sound in the stereo field (left to right)

EQ (Equalization) - Adjusting the balance of frequency components

Sampling - Using recordings of existing sounds in new compositions

Loop - Repeated section of music

Fade In/Out - Gradually increasing or decreasing volume at the beginning or end

Advanced Concepts

Modulation (Key Change) - Shifting from one key to another within a piece

Time Signature - The rhythmic framework (e.g., 4/4, 3/4, 6/8)

Cadence - A harmonic or melodic formula that creates a sense of resolution or pause

Ostinato - A repeated musical pattern or phrase

Pedal Point - A sustained or repeated note while harmonies change above it

Augmentation - Lengthening the rhythmic values of a melody

Diminution - Shortening the rhythmic values of a melody

Suspension - Holding a note from one chord into the next, creating tension

Anacrusis - Notes that occur before the first full measure (pickup notes)

Coda - A concluding section that brings a piece to an end

Tips for Using Musical Terms in Prompts
  • Combine tempo terms with genres (e.g., “upbeat allegro pop” or “slow adagio ballad”)
  • Use dynamics to shape emotional intensity (“crescendo into powerful chorus”)
  • Specify instrumentation for desired textures (“sparse piano and vocals” vs. “dense orchestral arrangement”)
  • Mix structural terms to guide song form (“verse-chorus-verse with extended bridge”)
  • Layer production effects for atmosphere (“reverb-heavy ambient soundscape”)
  • Blend genres creatively (“jazz-influenced hip-hop with soulful vocals”) The more specific your musical vocabulary, the more control you have over your Suno creations!

The Remaster feature allows you to create subtle variations of a clip, giving you more control over the final sound. It can be accessed from the Create menu.

Remaster is especially useful when you’re happy with the structure, lyrics, and performance of your track but want to refine how it sounds. You can use it to improve the overall sound quality, adjust the mix and balance of instruments and vocals, experiment with different sonic textures while keeping the same song, or even enhance the clarity and pronunciation in the vocals.

When you select Remaster on a song, you’ll see controls for the Model selector and Variation strength. Remaster includes control over the level of variation, giving you more flexibility in how closely the new clip matches the original.

Variation strength options

When using Remaster, you can choose the Variation strength to control how much the output differs from the original:

  • Subtle – Very close to the original; only small acoustic and production details are changed.

  • Normal (default) – Keeps duration and style the same while adding slight variations to musical details.

  • High – Creates more noticeable differences, including possible changes to musical elements and vocals. Here are some practical examples of when to use each Variation strength:

  • Subtle → Use this if you already like the track but want a slightly cleaner mix, smoother balance, or clearer pronunciation.

  • Normal → Choose this if you’d like the track to stay true to the original but with just enough variation to feel refreshed.

  • High → Select this if you want to experiment with noticeable twists, such as a new vocal tone, different instrument balance, or added creative details. How Remaster and Cover are related

Both Cover and Remaster generate variations of a clip, but the type and scale of changes are different:

  • Cover is designed for significant transformations. Use it when you want to change the vocalist, musical style, timing, or specific musical elements.
  • Remaster is best for subtle adjustments where the core structure, lyrics, and arrangement remain the same. It focuses on mix and audio production details such as:
    • Instrument balance
    • Audio effects
    • Sonic character and polish Note: Remaster is not intended for changing lyrics or drastically altering the musical style.

Voices: Use Your Voice in Suno

Edited

Voices lets you add your own voice to Suno-generated songs. Once your voice is set up, songs created with this feature will use your voice instead of a default Suno singer. This article walks through how to create a voice profile and use it when generating a song.

Before You Begin

Make sure you have an audio recording of your voice — either recorded in real time or as an existing audio file. Acapella recordings work best, but files with background music are also accepted (Suno will automatically isolate the vocal using stem splitting).

Step 1: Open the Add Voice Dialog

There are two ways to get started:

• Click the Add Voice button in Create.

• Click the Try Now button if it appears on your screen.

Both options open the same voice creation dialog.

Step 2: Create or Select a Voice

The dialog will show a list of any voice profiles you have previously created. You can either select an existing voice to reuse it, or create a new one by clicking Create Voice.

When creating a new voice, you have three options for the audio source:

• Use a voice from a song in your library — select a Suno song that already contains your voice.

• Record audio in real time — sing or speak directly into your microphone.

• Upload an existing audio file — upload a pre-recorded file of your voice from your device.

Step 3: Upload or Record Your Audio

Once you have selected your audio source, proceed to upload or record your clip. You can use an uploaded audio or audio recording from 15 seconds up to 4 minutes, then you can choose the best 2 minutes of your file. After uploading, you can preview the audio to confirm it captured your voice correctly.

Tip: Acapella recordings (voice only, no backing music) tend to produce the cleanest results along with a good microphone recorded in an acoustically neutral environment. However, if your file includes music, Suno will automatically run a stem extraction to isolate the vocal track.

When you are satisfied with the preview, click Use Voice to proceed.

Step 4: Verify Your Voice

Suno will display a short phrase for you to read aloud. This spoken recording is compared against your uploaded singing recording to confirm they are from the same person.

The system also verifies that you actually said the words shown. This step protects against the unauthorized use of other people’s voices, such as collaborators, creative partners, and public figures.

Speak the phrase clearly when prompted and wait for verification to complete.

Step 5: Complete Your Voice Profile

After verification, fill in the optional profile fields:

• Skill level — rate your vocal ability (e.g., beginner, intermediate, advanced).

• Profile image — optionally generate or upload an image to represent this voice.

• Voice name — give the profile a memorable name so you can find it easily later.

Check the confirmation box to affirm that you have the rights to use this voice, then click Save.

Step 6: Generate a Song with Your Voice

Your new voice profile is now loaded into the create form. To use it in future sessions, click Add Voice and select it from the top of the list.

From the create form:

  1. Paste in your lyrics.

  2. Add style descriptors (e.g., country ballad, acoustic).

  3. Enter a song title.

  4. Confirm the Voice model is selected in the model dropdown.

  5. Click Create. You’ll want to have the Audio Influence slider up fairly high.

Suno will generate two songs using your voice.

Automation in Studio

Automation in Studio

Edited

Overview

Automation lets you alter any parameter in your project over time. Think volume swells that build into a chorus, filter sweeps that open up a drop or delay throws that catch the tail of a vocal. Instead of setting a knob once and leaving it, you draw how it moves across your song.

How automation works

Any non-static plugin parameter can carry its own automation lane. That includes each track’s volume and pan, plus nearly every effect and plugin parameter

When a parameter is automated, its knob locks and shows a green outline. The knob then displays the live effective value, so you can always see exactly what the automation is doing at any point in playback.

How to use it
  • Press Shift+A (or click the Automation pill) to open the automation view for the focused track.
  • Right-click any knob and select Automate to create a lane for that parameter.
  • Double-click in the lane to add points. Drag points to move them, or drag the segments between points to bend the curve.
  • For fine edits, multi-select points and nudge them with the arrow keys.
  • During playback, the knob follows the lane automatically. To take back manual control, clear the lane.
Tips for best results
  • Start with volume. Automating a single track’s volume is the fastest way to learn the workflow before moving on to filters and effects.
  • Bend, don’t stack. Dragging a segment to curve it often sounds more natural than adding lots of points. To add a curve between two points, drag the point between two automation markers.
  • Watch the knob. The green outline tells you at a glance which parameters are automated, and the live value shows what the lane is doing in real time.
FAQs

Does automation apply when I export my song? Yes! Any automation you create in Studio will show up in your exported song and will be present on download stems.

How do I remove automation from a parameter? Right click the parameter you automated and select “Delete automation lane”. You can also delete specific automation points on the automation lane. Simply select a portion of the lane with the point(s) you want to delete and hit delete.

Can I copy automation from one track or parameter to another? Absolutely. Duplicating a track with automation will still have the automation from the track you duplicated it from. You can also copy and paste automation points on an automation lane by highlighting what points in the lane you want to copy and paste them at your desired destination along the automation lane.

MIDI Tracks

MIDI tracks live on the timeline right next to your audio tracks. The difference is what’s inside: their clips hold notes instead of recorded sound. Those notes get sent to whatever instrument is loaded in the track’s effects rack, which turns them into audio.

Everything you can do with an audio track works here too: set a color, mute and solo, arm for recording, use take lanes, duplicate and reorder.

Every new MIDI track ships with the Wavetable synth already loaded. Tap a musical typing key and you’ll hear it instantly, no setup needed.

Create a MIDI track in Studio
  • Click + Track in the footer below your last track, or press Shift+T.
  • In the “Add a track” modal, pick MIDI. Click it, use the arrow keys and Enter, or just press M.
  • Your new track appears with a basic synth patch ready to go. (You can choose another preset to change the sound.)
  • Open the playable keyboard (Cmd+K) or plug in a MIDI controller and start playing.
Add notes by hand
  • Select a region of blank space on the MIDI track and click + Add Midi Clip to create a clip.
  • Double-click the empty clip header to open the piano roll.
  • Double-click inside the piano roll to place notes.
Piano Roll

The piano roll is where you edit the notes inside a MIDI clip. It’s a grid: a piano keyboard runs down the left side to show pitch, and time flows left to right in bars and beats. Each note appears as a block you can place, stretch and move, giving you precise control over your melodies, chords and rhythms.

How to use it
  • Double-click any MIDI clip.
  • Draw notes. Switch to draw pointer mode (hold Alt to flip between select and draw) or double-click anywhere to place a note.
  • Edit notes. Drag notes to move them, drag their edges to resize.
  • Quantize. Select some notes and press Shift+Q to snap them to the grid.
  • Adjust velocities. Click the Automation toggle in the lower right to open the velocity panel, then drag the stalks in the lane below the grid to change how hard each note hits.
  • Stay in key. Turn on scale assist (the key/scale control in the clip footer) to highlight the rows that belong to your key.
Musical Typing and the Playable Keyboard

No MIDI controller? No problem. Musical typing turns your computer keyboard into one. Studio uses the standard DAW homerow layout, which maps your keys to a piano:

  • A through ; play the white keys.
  • W, E, T, Y, U, O, P play the black keys.
  • Z and X shift the octave down and up.
  • C and V lower and raise velocity. Whatever you play routes to the armed or focused MIDI track, so you can jam live and record on the spot.
Playable modes and pattern tools

The floating Synth panel builds on raw typing with four playable modes plus pattern tools:

  • Keyboard. The classic homerow piano.
  • Arpeggiator: Play complex rhythmic patterns with one finger. Any chord you play can be dragged straight onto the timeline, where it lands as a MIDI clip.
How to use it
  • Focus or arm a MIDI track.
  • Press Cmd+K or click the keyboard icon at the bottom right to open the playable keyboard.
  • Make sure an instrument is loaded on the track.
  • Play with your typing keyboard. Use Z/X to move octaves and C/V to adjust velocity.
  • To capture what you play, arm the track and hit record (Shift+R).
External MIDI Input, MIDI Learn and Transport Control

Hardware MIDI controllers connect through the browser’s Web MIDI API, so there are no drivers to install. Note on/off messages play and record into any armed MIDI track, and a per-track input selector lets you choose which device feeds which track.

Beyond playing notes, your controller can do three more things:

  • MIDI Learn: Map the knobs and faders on your hardware to controls in Studio. Right-click any plugin or instrument knob, choose MIDI Learn and move a hardware knob. The binding sticks and saves with the project, and a single physical knob can drive multiple parameters at once.
  • Transport control: Run playback from your controller’s buttons. Unmapped CCs 115 to 118 default to play, stop, record and loop (the Novation convention), and Start/Continue/Stop messages from clock-master controllers are respected too.
  • MIDI recording: Notes are captured into takes, and those takes comp across loop passes just like audio takes do.
How to use it
  • Plug in your controller and grant the browser MIDI permission when prompted.
  • Pick the device in the MIDI track’s input selector, found in the track header.
  • Play. The track’s instrument responds in real time. Arm the track and hit record to capture your performance.
  • To bind a knob, right-click a parameter, choose MIDI Learn and move the hardware control you want to use.
  • If your controller sends CC 115 to 118, its transport buttons drive play, stop, record and loop automatically.
What is MIDI?

MIDI is an acronym that stands for Musical Instrument Digital Interface. A technical standard enabling electronic instruments and computers to communicate that has been around since 1983 and is still heavily used today including in Suno Studio!

How does MIDI work?

Even though MIDI is instrumental in the music production process; MIDI does not actually make sound. Rather it sends over information called MIDI Messages. Simply put, these MIDI messages are sent over to a device that receives those messages to command what that device should do like a keyboard controller playing a hardware synthesizer, a drum machine syncing tempo to a looper, or a computer sending note data to an external sound module.

What is a MIDI controller?

As mentioned above, MIDI messages being sent over to communicate with hardware devices is still key to modern music production. A MIDI controller in the modern production era is most often used as a MIDI controller being connected to a computer via USB; with that controller sending information to a digital audio workstation. Different types of MIDI controllers include traditional piano style MIDI keyboards, DJ Controllers, Wind Controllers, Pad Controllers, and MIDI control surfaces.

Studio Chat

Overview

The chat bar (currently in beta) is how you talk to Studio. Instead of hunting through menus, you type what you want in plain language (“make the drums punchier”, “add a moody synth pad under the chorus”, “give me three alternate takes of this section”) and Studio makes the change. Chat sees your project, understands what you have selected and edits using the same operations you would.

Why it matters

Traditional DAWs ask you to know which tool does the job before you can use it, which can make production hard to learn and slows even seasoned producers outside their familiar workflows. The chat bar removes that friction between inspiration and execution.

If you’re new to production, chat is your on-ramp. You can make real multitrack music from day one by describing what you hear in your head, and pick up the underlying concepts (tracks, takes, effects chains) by watching chat work on your project.

If you’re an experienced producer, chat is a speed layer. Routine, describable work like laying down a scratch part, setting up an effects chain or generating variations becomes a one-line request, so your hands and attention stay on the creative decisions.

Your first request

To open the chat bar, click on the circular sparkle icon in the middle of the screen, or select a track/clip and . press Enter . It expands into a full text bar, cursor ready. Type something like “add a laid-back bass line that follows the drums”.

Chat narrates its work in short, live status lines (“Inspecting the tracks”), and a new bass track appears on the timeline with a generated part. When it’s done, chat replies with what it did and, where useful, leaves buttons anchored to its message:

  • Audition Take 2 plays an alternative in place, and flips to Commit if you want to keep it.
  • Add to track appears when chat has built you a custom effect. If your request is ambiguous, chat asks before guessing. A question like “Should the bass follow the kick pattern or play half-time?” appears as a poll with tappable options and waits for your answer.

You can toggle between the floating chat bar and the left-side chat dialog by pressing the 1 key.

Good to know
  • Everything is undoable. Chat edits through the same pipeline as your own actions, so Cmd+Z undoes a chat change exactly like one of yours. Nothing it does is a black box you can’t step back from.
  • It sees what you see. Every request is grounded in your current context: the focused track, your selection, the project’s tempo. “Make this louder” refers to the thing you have selected.
  • Easy to conjure and dismiss. Enter summons the prompt from anywhere and Escape dismisses it in a predictable cascade.
  • Your conversation sticks around. History persists per project, so tomorrow’s session picks up with yesterday’s context. You can clear it anytime from the settings menu.
Two surfaces, one conversation

The same conversation shows up in two places:

  • The Magic Bar. The floating pill/bar over the timeline. This is the quick-command form: conjure, type, submit and get back to the music.
  • The docked chat panel. A full-height panel on the left that opens when you submit a prompt (or press the scope control). This is where you review conversation history. Both surfaces drive one shared session, so nothing is lost moving between them.
What chat can do
  • Read your project. List tracks, inspect a track’s contents and settings, and see your selection and tempo.
  • Edit tracks and clips. The same structural edits you can make by hand, all attributed and undoable.
  • Generate music. Compose MIDI parts and generate alternate audio takes for a section you’ve selected.
  • Build effects. Author a custom plugin to match a described sound (“a warm tape saturation”) and offer it as an Add to track button. Longer builds continue in the background so you can keep working.
  • Navigate for you. Open the right view for the task, like jumping to a track’s effects rack, rather than telling you where to click.
  • Ask before guessing. Pause mid-task to ask a clarifying question with one-tap answers.

The Wavetable Synth in Studio

Studio

Studio 2.0

The Wavetable Synth in Studio

Edited

Wavetable is Studio’s flagship instrument. It generates sound by moving through a table of single-cycle waveforms, and that one technique covers a huge range of territory. Deep basses, cutting leads, lush pads, tight plucks and slowly evolving textures all come out of the same engine.

The Wavetable synth can be set up and controlled manually, and you can even map parameters to physical controls on an external USB/MIDI keyboard. However, thanks to Suno Studio’s chat bar, you can also prompt synth settings without touching the knobs.

To do this, create a MIDI track, select the track header and open the chat bar (tip: press the Enter key). Then use natural language to describe the kind of instrument you want to create. Like “create a glassy synth pad that evolves into a wobble.”

Under the hood, Wavetable gives you:

Oscillators

  • Oscillator 1 & 2: Each plays back a wavetable (a collection of single-cycle waveforms); you can scan through the table to morph the timbre continuously.

  • Warp: Per-oscillator warp mode that distorts the playback position within the wavetable, adding extra harmonic content and movement.

  • Unison: Stacks multiple detuned voices per oscillator to thicken and widen the sound.

  • Spread: Controls the stereo width of the unison voices — wider spread = bigger, more spatial sound. Sub Oscillator

  • Sub Osc: A dedicated low-frequency sine (or square) oscillator one octave below Osc 1, great for adding weight and punch to basses and leads without muddying the midrange. Filter

  • Multimode Filter: Switchable filter type (low-pass, high-pass, band-pass, notch, etc.) that shapes the harmonic content of the oscillator mix.

  • Cutoff: Sets the frequency at which the filter starts attenuating; sweeping it is the classic synth “opening up” sound.

  • Resonance: Boosts frequencies around the cutoff point; at high values it can self-oscillate into a sine-wave tone. Envelopes (×4)

  • Amp Envelope: Controls the volume shape over time: Attack, Decay, Sustain, Release — how the note blooms and fades.

  • Filter Envelope: Modulates the filter cutoff over time, for plucks, stabs, and sweeping timbral movement.

  • Pitch/Aux Envelopes: Two additional envelopes freely assignable via the mod matrix to any parameter (pitch, warp, LFO rate, etc.). LFOs (×3)

  • LFO 1–3: Low Frequency Oscillators with selectable shapes (sine, triangle, square, saw, random); they cycle slowly to create vibrato, tremolo, filter wobble, or any rhythmic modulation. Can be tempo-synced. Modulation Matrix

  • Mod Matrix: A flexible routing grid where any source (envelope, LFO, velocity, mod wheel, etc.) can be connected to any destination parameter with a scalable amount — the heart of the synth’s expressiveness and movement. Global / Voice

  • Polyphony / Mono mode — Sets whether the synth plays chords or is monophonic (one note at a time), with legato/glide options for smooth pitch slides between notes.

  • Glide (Portamento) — Slides pitch smoothly from one note to the next; essential for bass lines and leads.

  • Octave / Semitone / Fine Tune — Per-oscillator pitch controls for transposing or detuning relative to each other. Presets

  • Factory presets: Spanning 808-style basses, Reese basses, acid lines, pads, plucks, retro game sounds, and more — a good starting point for any of the above.

How to use it
  • Create a MIDI track (Shift+T, then choose MIDI or press M). Wavetable loads on the track automatically.
  • Click the Wavetable device at the bottom left to open its compact controls. To open the full editor, click Resize in the upper right or double click the device header.
  • Pick a starting point from the preset dropdown, then start tweaking. Every knob responds in real time while you play.
  • Play notes however you like: the typing keyboard (Cmd+K), the chord pads or a connected MIDI controller.

Overview

The EQ (Equalizer) is a powerful audio processing tool that allows you to shape the tonal character of your sound by adjusting specific frequency bands. This graphic equalizer with additional parametric controls provide precise control over your audio spectrum with multiple adjustable bands.

Interface Components
EQ Toggle

Located in the top-left corner, the EQ toggle switch enables or disables the equalizer processing. When enabled (blue), the EQ actively processes your audio signal. To access the EQ for your track, select the track you want to use the EQ on, then click Track in the Details Panel.

Preset Management
  • Preset Display: Shows the current preset name (displayed as “[Custom]” when manually adjusted)
  • Navigation Arrows: Browse through available presets using the left and right arrow buttons
Frequency Response Graph

The visual display shows your EQ curve in real-time:

  • Horizontal axis: Represents the frequency spectrum (low to high frequencies)
  • Vertical axis: Represents gain (boost or cut in dB)
  • Blue line: Your current EQ curve
  • Control points: Draggable nodes that represent each active band
Band Selection

The equalizer offers 6 selectable bands (numbered 1-6). Active bands are highlighted in blue, allowing you to adjust multiple frequency ranges simultaneously. You can enable or disable individual bands as needed.

Filter Types

Six different filter shapes are available (shown as wave icons):

Bell/Peak (first icon - selected in image): Boosts or cuts a specific frequency range

High-pass: Removes low frequencies below the set point

Low-pass: Removes high frequencies above the set point

High-shelf: Boosts or cuts all frequencies above the set point

Low-shelf: Boosts or cuts all frequencies below the set point

: Creates a narrow cut at a specific frequency

Adjustable Parameters
Freq (Frequency)
  • Range: Displayed as “60Hz” in the example
  • Function: Sets the center frequency for the selected band
  • Rotate the dial to select which frequency range you want to adjust
  • Lower values affect bass, mid-range values affect vocals and instruments, higher values affect brightness and air
Gain
  • Range: Displayed as “-0.4dB” in the example
  • Function: Boosts (+) or cuts (-) the volume at the selected frequency
  • Positive values enhance the selected frequency range
  • Negative values reduce the selected frequency range
  • Typical range: -12dB to +12dB
Res (Resonance/Q Factor)
  • Range: Displayed as “0.71” in the example
  • Function: Controls the width of the frequency band being affected
  • Lower values create a wider, more gentle curve
  • Higher values create a narrower, more precise adjustment
  • Use narrow Q for surgical corrections, wider Q for musical shaping
Tips for Use

Getting Started:

  • Enable the EQ using the top toggle switch

  • Select a band (1-6) you want to adjust

  • Choose an appropriate filter type for your needs

  • Adjust Freq to target the desired frequency range

  • Use Gain to boost or cut as needed

  • Fine-tune Res to control how wide or narrow the effect is Best Practices:

  • Start with subtle adjustments (±3dB) and increase only if needed

  • Use cuts rather than boosts when possible for more natural results

  • Enable only the bands you’re actively using

  • Use the visual graph to ensure your EQ curve is smooth and musical

  • Save useful settings as presets for quick recall Common Applications:

  • Remove muddiness by cutting around 200-400Hz

  • Add presence by boosting around 2-5kHz

  • Reduce harshness by cutting around 3-4kHz

  • Add air and sparkle by gently boosting above 10kHz

  • Control bass by adjusting below 100Hz

  • The timeline shows your project’s time range
  • The recording will loop over the visible/selected section
  • Adjust your loop endpoints as needed before recording

Starting the Recording
  • Press the Record button in the transport controls
  • The track displays “RECORDING…” in red/dark red
  • Playback begins and the loop starts
During Loop Recording

First Pass:

  • When the loop reaches the end, it automatically returns to the start

Tempo controls the overall speed of the Project. Tempo is measured in Beats Per Minute, or BPM. Like most DAWs, Studio defaults to 120 BPM when opening a new project. Clicking on Tempo allows you to set the project’s BPM. You can also select Follow Track, which makes the Tempo follow the natural tempo of regions on the timeline, or select Manual Tempo, which allows Studio to set a fixed tempo for the entire project and automatically adjusts the BPM of individual regions to match the project’s tempo.

Metronome

The Metronome is a built-in timing tool that produces regular clicking sounds at the project’s tempo, helping musicians stay in time during recording and production. Simply click the Metronome to disable and enable it.

Record

Record enables the capture of sound from external sources (microphones, instruments, or line inputs) and converts it into digital audio files within Studio on the Timeline. To record audio, an Audio Track must have an Input with an active signal selected, and recording must be enabled by clicking the corresponding Record button on the track itself.

Back to Section Start

Back to Section Start allows you to quickly return to the beginning of regions on the Timeline. You can also use Back to Section Start when you’ve listened to your entire song and want to return to the very beginning.

Play/Pause

Play and Pause are the fundamental transport controls that start and stop playback of your project. Play starts playback from the current playhead position and Pause temporarily stops playback while maintaining current position.

Forward to Section Start

Like Back to Section Start, Forward to Section Start lets you navigate forward on the Timeline to the Start and End of regions.

Loop

Loop allows you to continuously repeat a section of your project, letting you focus on specific areas without manually restarting playback. To enable looping, select a section in the timeline and click Loop.

Follow Playhead

Follow Playhead automatically scrolls the timeline to keep the playhead visible during playback, ensuring you can always see the current playback position as the project plays.

Clock

The Clock by default shows the beat and count where the playhead is currently positioned on the Timeline. Clicking on the Clock allows you to switch to Time display in Minutes:Seconds:Milliseconds format to show the playhead’s position in the Timeline.

How do I get stems?

Edited

Stems let you break a song into separate musical parts — vocals, drums, bass, guitar, keys, and more — so you can work with each one individually.

From your Workspace or Library

  1. Select the More Actions (…) menu next to any song

  2. Select Get Stems

  3. Choose a split type

  4. Select Extract

Note: Get Stems is available to Pro and Premier subscribers.

From Suno Studio

  1. Right-click an audio clip on the timeline

  2. Select Split Stems

  3. Choose a split type

  4. Select Extract

What is Advanced Stem Separation?

Advanced Stem Separation breaks a song down with more precision and cleaner results, with three modes:

Auto Split — Separates into up to 12 stems. Available on Pro and Premier. Costs 50 credits per extraction.

Split from Mix — Extracts a single instrument or vocal, plus a complement track. Available on Pro and Premier. Costs 10 credits per stem.

Advanced Split — You choose which stems to separate from a list of nearly 100 instruments. Premier only. Costs 10 credits per stem.

Note: Advanced Split is available to Premier subscribers only.
